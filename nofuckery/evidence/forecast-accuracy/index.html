Verdict

Aggregation level, not forecast quality, can move an accuracy number by an order of magnitude. RELEX’s own guide illustrates the same dataset at 3% or 33%. The published figures across this category do not say which level produced them.

Accountable editor: Chris McNosky · No commercial relationship with any vendor named · Primary records read: 2026-08-05

Scope: four published claims from two vendors. This is a measurement argument, not a product verdict.

Why the number moves

Forecast accuracy is not a property of a forecast alone. It is a property of a forecast and the level at which error is calculated. Compute the error on aggregated group totals and offsetting item-level misses cancel each other out. Compute it per item and then average, and they do not.

RELEX’s guide puts the spread at roughly tenfold on one dataset — 3% against 33% — and is explicit that the two are not comparable quantities. That is a correct and careful statement of the problem, published by a vendor, in public.

The difficulty is what happens next. A published figure such as “89%” or “12% improvement” carries no indication of which calculation produced it. A buyer comparing two vendors’ headline numbers is, in the guide’s own words, comparing quantities that “should never be compared.”

Why it matters operationally

Aggregation level is not a reporting preference. It determines whether the number describes the decision being automated. Replenishment decisions are made per item, per location. An accuracy figure calculated at regional or category level can be accurate as arithmetic and still say almost nothing about the quality of the decisions the system will actually make.

Where forecast output drives automated replenishment, the accuracy claim is the load-bearing assumption underneath a system that commits inventory without a human in the loop.

Strongest countercase

Marketing pages are not technical documentation, and no reasonable buyer signs on the basis of a website figure. Detailed methodology is normally exchanged under NDA during evaluation, where these questions do get asked. On that view, the absence of methodology on a public page is a genre convention rather than a defect.

That countercase is largely right about procurement and does not rescue the numbers. Public figures set the shortlist, anchor the expectation, and are quoted back in competitive comparisons long before any NDA exists. A quantity that cannot be interpreted still moves the decision. RELEX’s own guide makes the narrower point unavoidable: if the two calculations “should never be compared,” then publishing one without saying which invites exactly the comparison the vendor has already ruled out.

What this does not prove

  • It does not establish that any figure cited here is false. Each may be entirely accurate.
  • It does not establish that any vendor calculated at a flattering level. No aggregation level is disclosed, which is the finding.
  • It does not establish that any product performs better or worse than another.
  • It does not establish that undisclosed methodology exists or does not exist behind these pages.
  • It does not examine o9, Kinaxis, SAP IBP, ToolsGroup, or Manhattan Associates. Their absence is a limit of this brief, not a finding about them.
  • It does not rest on any test, benchmark, or reproduction run by NoFuckery AI. No forecast was evaluated here.
  • The 3% and 33% figures are RELEX’s illustrative example, not a measured result from a named customer. The order-of-magnitude effect they illustrate is separately asserted by RELEX as fact; the specific figures are not.
  • It does not establish that detailed methodology is withheld during procurement. It addresses published figures only.
  • This brief was written and reviewed by the same person. It has not received independent adversarial review.

Five questions before signing

  1. Which metric produced this number — MAPE, WMAPE, bias, or something else?
  2. At what aggregation level was it calculated: item/location/day, or DC/region/week?
  3. What is the baseline — the prior system, or a naive forecast?
  4. Over what period, and does it include peak season?
  5. Would you restate this at the level the replenishment decision is made — and if percentage error is unstable there, which metric do you use instead?

The fifth question is the one that settles it. The first four can be answered by anyone. The fifth asks whether the headline survives contact with the level at which inventory decisions are actually made.

The second half of that question matters. MAPE degrades badly on intermittent and slow-moving demand, where small absolute errors produce enormous percentages and near-zero actuals break the calculation entirely — RELEX’s own guide makes this point. So “restate it at item level” is not a demand for item-level MAPE. It is a demand that the vendor name a metric that remains meaningful at the level where the system actually commits stock. A vendor who cannot is describing a number that does not correspond to the decision being automated.

Operator decision

Treat a forecast-accuracy percentage without a stated metric, aggregation level, baseline, and period as uninterpretable rather than impressive. Require the aggregation level in writing before a number enters a comparison, and restate every competing figure at the level the decision is made.
